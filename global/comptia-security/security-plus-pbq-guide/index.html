How to Solve Security+ Performance-Based Questions

By Prostutii Editorial Team • Last Verified: 2026-11-18
⚡Direct Answer / Summary
Security+ performance-based questions (PBQs) ask you to complete a task in a simulation, such as matching controls, ordering firewall rules, or reading a log, instead of picking an answer. Identify the goal, do the parts you are sure of first, check your work against the constraints, and use CompTIA’s option to skip and return.

Last verified: November 18, 2026, against CompTIA’s PBQ overview, its PBQ help article and the V7 and V8 exam pages.

Performance-based questions worry more Security+ candidates than any other part of the exam, mostly because they look unfamiliar. They are less mysterious than they seem. Each one gives you a small, realistic task and a clear goal, and the same method works every time. This guide explains what CompTIA says about PBQs, gives you a five-step method, and walks you through four original practice formats with worked solutions.

Key takeaways
✦ The exam is a maximum of 90 questions in 90 minutes, a mix of multiple-choice questions and PBQs.
✦ CompTIA says simulation PBQs can be skipped and returned to later, and that your work is saved.
✦ Some PBQs may offer partial credit and some may not, so complete every part you can.
✦ Read the constraints first: ordering, “first match wins,” and what the task says to optimize.
✦ Practice formats, not real questions. Exam dumps are against CompTIA’s rules and teach answers without understanding.

What are performance-based questions?

According to CompTIA, performance-based questions are exam items designed to test your ability to solve problems in real-world settings. They are delivered as simulations or within virtual environments. A simulation is an approximation of an environment or tool, such as a firewall, a network diagram, a terminal window or an operating system, with restricted functionality and, usually, more than one valid way to reach a correct result.

CompTIA’s help center lists Security+ among the exams that include simulation PBQs. Here is what CompTIA says about how they work:

TopicWhat CompTIA states
Exam formatSY0-701 and SY0-801 have a maximum of 90 questions, a mix of multiple-choice and performance-based questions, in 90 minutes.
SkippingYou can skip a simulation PBQ and return to it later, and your work is saved when you move to another item.
InstructionsThe instructions stay available in a simulation PBQ and can be minimized, moved and resized.
ResetThe Reset button in a simulation resets that question only.
Partial creditSome PBQs may offer partial credit and others may not. Some may be worth more points than others. Scoring is confidential.
Multiple pathsThere may be more than one way to reach a correct answer.

CompTIA does not say how many PBQs appear on an exam or where they fall, so ignore any site that claims a fixed number. You can try a sample simulation on CompTIA’s PBQ page. CompTIA states that its samples are not from any real exam, and so are the practice formats in this guide.

A five-step method for any PBQ

  1. Read the goal and every constraint. Underline words such as “only,” “first,” “most restrictive,” and any rule about ordering.
  2. List the parts. Count the items to place, rules to order or fields to set so you know what “done” looks like.
  3. Do the sure parts first. Because some PBQs may award partial credit, completing what you know is better than leaving a task blank.
  4. Check your work against the constraints. Walk a few sample cases through your answer, as the firewall example below does.
  5. Decide and move on. If you are stuck, move on. Your work is saved, and you can return to a simulation PBQ later.

Time management: when to do PBQs

You have 90 minutes for a maximum of 90 questions, so an average of one minute per question works only if some items go faster than others. Simulation PBQs usually take longer than multiple-choice questions. Because CompTIA allows you to skip and return to a simulation PBQ, many candidates use a three-pass approach. This is a strategy, not an official rule, so test it in practice before exam day.

  1. First pass: answer the multiple-choice questions you know, and flag the rest.
  2. Second pass: work through the PBQs with a personal time cap. A common starting point is about 8 minutes each, and you should adjust it from your practice results.
  3. Third pass: return to flagged questions and any PBQ you left partly complete.
Pitfall: spending 20 minutes on the first PBQ.
A single stubborn simulation can eat the time you need for five easier questions. Set the cap before you start and stick to it. If the question has parts you cannot do, complete the parts you can and move on.

Practice format 1: match controls to scenarios

Drag-and-drop matching is the simplest PBQ style. You place the correct item in each slot. Some items in the bank are distractors that match nothing.

Task: Match each scenario to the BEST control. Three controls will not be used.

Scenarios:
S1. Staff keep emailing customer lists to personal accounts, and the company wants to detect and block sensitive data leaving.
S2. Remote employees must prove their identity with something besides a password.
S3. A lost laptop must not expose customer data.
S4. Signs and bright lighting are added at the loading dock to discourage trespassing.
S5. A failed software update must be reversed quickly.

Control bank: Data loss prevention (DLP), multifactor authentication, full-disk encryption, deterrent controls, backout plan, network segmentation, intrusion detection system, vulnerability scan.
Show the worked solution

S1 to DLP: it detects and blocks sensitive data leaving. S2 to multifactor authentication: it adds a second factor. S3 to full-disk encryption: it keeps data unreadable on a lost device. S4 to deterrent controls: signs and lighting discourage rather than detect or repair. S5 to backout plan: it describes how to return to the previous state.

Why the leftovers do not fit: network segmentation limits movement between systems, an intrusion detection system only detects and alerts, and a vulnerability scan finds weaknesses. None of the scenarios asks for those outcomes.

Practice format 2: order the firewall rules

Rule-ordering tasks test whether you understand that order matters. In this practice format, rules are evaluated from top to bottom, and the first matching rule decides the packet’s fate. Real firewalls differ in details, so on the exam follow whatever behavior the question states.

Goal: The web server at 203.0.113.10 must accept HTTPS from anyone, accept SSH only from the admin network 198.51.100.0/24, and block everything else. Put the four rules in the correct order.
RuleActionProtocol and portSourceDestination
ADenyAnyAnyAny
BAllowTCP 443Any203.0.113.10
CAllowTCP 22198.51.100.0/24203.0.113.10
DDenyTCP 22Any203.0.113.10
Show the worked solution

Correct order: C, D, B, A. Put the specific rules first and the broad rule last. C must come before D. If D came first, it would block the admins before C could allow them. A must be last, because it matches everything and would stop any rule below it from ever being used.

Check your order by tracing packets:

PacketRule that matches firstResult
198.51.100.25 to 203.0.113.10, TCP 22CAllowed
192.0.2.50 to 203.0.113.10, TCP 22D (C does not match the source)Denied
192.0.2.50 to 203.0.113.10, TCP 443BAllowed
192.0.2.50 to 203.0.113.10, TCP 3389ADenied

Rule B can sit anywhere above A, because no other rule overlaps with HTTPS. Rule D is partly redundant with A, but making it explicit is common practice and keeps the intent clear.

Practice format 3: read the log and respond

Log tasks ask you to find the pattern, name the attack and choose a response. Read the same five things every time: who, what, when, how often and from where.

Mar 14 03:12:01 srv1 sshd[2210]: Failed password for root from 198.51.100.23 port 52211 ssh2
Mar 14 03:12:02 srv1 sshd[2212]: Failed password for root from 198.51.100.23 port 52213 ssh2
Mar 14 03:12:03 srv1 sshd[2214]: Failed password for root from 198.51.100.23 port 52215 ssh2
...      (41 more failed attempts for root from the same address in about two minutes)
Mar 14 03:13:49 srv1 sshd[2301]: Accepted password for root from 198.51.100.23 port 52390 ssh2
Mar 14 03:13:50 srv1 sshd[2301]: session opened for user root
Tasks: (a) Name the attack. (b) Did it succeed? (c) What is the BEST immediate action? (d) Which controls would prevent a repeat?
Show the worked solution

(a) Brute-force attack against one account. One source made many rapid guesses at the same account, root. Spraying would instead show one attempt each against many different accounts.

(b) Yes. The “Accepted password” line after a long run of failures shows the attacker guessed the password and opened a session as root.

(c) Contain first. End the session and block the source address, then preserve the logs and investigate what the attacker did as root. Treat it as an incident, and reset the credential after containment.

(d) Prevent a repeat. Disable direct root login over SSH, use key-based authentication or multifactor authentication, rate-limit or temporarily lock after repeated failures, restrict SSH to known addresses, and alert on bursts of failed logins.

Practice format 4: configure a basic policy

Configuration tasks give you a scenario and a set of settings to choose. The right values depend on the stated requirements, so read them closely.

Scenario: A company with remote staff wants to make online password guessing impractical, without letting attackers lock out legitimate users and without constant help desk calls. Choose one value for each setting.
SettingOptions
1. Minimum password length6 characters, 8 characters, 14 characters
2. Multifactor authentication for remote accessOptional, required, disabled
3. Failed-attempt handlingNo limit, lock for 15 minutes after 5 failures, lock permanently after 1 failure
4. Password reuseAllow immediate reuse, block reuse of recent passwords, require a change every day
5. Shared administrator accountAllowed for convenience, prohibited with individual accounts and logging
Show the worked solution

1. 14 characters. Longer passwords are harder to guess. 2. Required. A second factor stops a stolen or guessed password from being enough. 3. Lock for 15 minutes after 5 failures. It slows guessing, while a permanent lock after one failure would let an attacker deliberately lock out real users, which creates the help desk calls the company wants to avoid. “No limit” invites guessing.

4. Block reuse of recent passwords. It prevents cycling back to a compromised password, while forcing a daily change would push people toward weak patterns. 5. Prohibit shared administrator accounts. Individual accounts with logging give you accountability, the “accounting” part of AAA.

Common PBQ mistakes

  • Skimming the instructions. The constraint that decides the answer is often one short phrase.
  • Leaving a task empty. Some PBQs may award partial credit, so complete the parts you can.
  • Assuming one correct path. CompTIA says there may be more than one way to a correct result, so do not freeze when your approach differs from a tutorial’s.
  • Not checking the result. Trace sample cases through your rule order or settings before you move on.
  • Studying leaked questions. Exam dumps break CompTIA’s rules and do not build the skill PBQs test.
  • Overusing Reset. It clears only that question, but resetting wastes the progress you already made.

Pre-exam PBQ checklist

  • ☐ I tried CompTIA’s sample simulation on its PBQ page.
  • ☐ I can match controls and tools to scenarios without a word bank.
  • ☐ I can order rules and trace sample packets through them.
  • ☐ I can read a short log and name the pattern and the first response.
  • ☐ I can choose settings that meet every stated requirement.
  • ☐ I know I can skip a simulation PBQ and return to it.
  • ☐ I have a time cap and a pass order I tested in practice.
