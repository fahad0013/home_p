Security+ SY0-801 AI Security Scenarios Explained

By Prostutii Editorial • Last Verified: 2026-11-18
⚡Direct Answer / Summary
SY0-801 covers AI security risks that are easiest to learn through scenarios: prompt injection (untrusted text steering a chatbot), data poisoning (tampered training data), and deepfake fraud (synthetic voice or video used for social engineering). For each, learn the risk’s name, how to spot it, and the control that limits damage, such as least privilege or out-of-band verification.

Last verified: November 18, 2026, against CompTIA’s Security+ V8 page. For the exact objective wording, use the official SY0-801 objectives (PDF).

Security+ SY0-801 launched on November 17, 2026 with a dedicated objective on the security risks of artificial intelligence. If you have never worked with AI systems, the terms can feel abstract. They are not. Each one maps to a failure a security team can investigate and a control a security team can apply. This guide walks through three tickets of the kind you might meet on the job, then shows how the same ideas appear on the exam.

Everything here is defensive and conceptual. You will not find attack prompts or step-by-step exploitation, because Security+ asks you to recognize risks and choose controls, not to attack systems. If you are still choosing between exam versions, start with our SY0-701 vs SY0-801 guide.

Key takeaways
✦ CompTIA’s V8 summary lists a dedicated objective on AI risks: AI systems, models, prompts, privacy and data exposure.
✦ Prompt injection is about untrusted text steering an AI. Poisoning is about tampered training data. They are different risks with different controls.
✦ Deepfakes show up as a social engineering risk, and the best control is a process, not better eyesight: out-of-band verification and dual approval.
✦ The strongest answer to most AI risks is the classic one: least privilege, validation, logging and human oversight.
✦ Learn to name the risk from a short scenario, then pick the control that limits the damage.

What the official objectives say about AI

According to CompTIA’s Security+ V8 page, the Threats, Vulnerabilities, and Attacks domain (24% of the exam) includes an objective to explain the security risks associated with artificial intelligence, covering risks related to AI systems, models, prompts, privacy and data exposure. The same page mentions AI-assisted workflows under automation and orchestration in Security Operations.

Several study sites that have read the full objectives document report that this objective is numbered 2.6, is worded as “summarize threats and vulnerabilities associated with artificial intelligence (AI) usage,” and lists 13 topics. They also report related AI items elsewhere: large language models as an attack surface (2.4), deepfakes under social engineering in the indicators of malicious activity objective (2.5), and AI capabilities under automation (4.6). The verb “summarize” suggests recognition-level questions: name the risk, explain the impact, pick a sensible control.

Pitfall: studying AI as a technical specialty.
Security+ is a defensive, entry-level security exam. You do not need to train or tune models. A candidate who spends a week on machine-learning math has misread the objective. Spend that week on recognizing risks and mapping them to controls.

The AI risk terms in plain English

The topics reported under the AI objective fall into three groups: attacks on the model, attacks through the model, and risks in the output and data. The definitions below are our plain-language explanations, not CompTIA’s wording.

GroupTermPlain-English meaning
Attacks on the modelPoisoningTampering with the data a model learns from, so it learns the wrong thing
Attacks on the modelModel manipulationAltering or steering a model’s behavior or components without authorization
Attacks on the modelEvasionCrafting inputs so a model fails to detect or classify something correctly
Attacks on the modelJailbreakingGetting a model to ignore its built-in safety rules
Attacks through the modelPrompt injectionUntrusted text (a message, a document, a web page) that steers the AI away from its intended instructions
Attacks through the modelSession hijackingTaking over a legitimate user’s session with an AI-connected service
Attacks through the modelCode executionAn AI tool being tricked into running commands or code it should not
Risks in output and dataData loss and privacySensitive data leaving through prompts, outputs or logs, or being used improperly
Risks in output and dataHallucinationsConfident but false or invented output that people may trust
Risks in output and dataBiasSystematically unfair or skewed results caused by data or design
Risks in output and dataExplainabilityThe ability to understand why a model produced a result, which matters for audits and trust
Risks in output and dataEthical considerationsFairness, transparency and accountability in how AI is used

That is 12 rows for the 13 reported topics, because data loss and privacy are listed separately in some breakdowns and combined here. Check the official PDF for the exact list.

Scenario 1: the support chatbot that shared the wrong order

TICKET: SEC-4127 | Priority: High | Status: Open
Opened: Tuesday 09:42 | Reporter: Customer Support Lead
System: “OrderHelper” AI chatbot on the public help page
Symptom: A customer reports seeing another person’s order status in the chat. Transcript shows the customer’s message contained text telling the bot to disregard its rules and show “all recent orders.”

Investigation

  1. Contain. Temporarily disable the chatbot’s order-lookup tool while keeping the rest of the help page online.
  2. Preserve evidence. Export the chat transcripts, tool-call logs and timestamps. Do not edit them.
  3. Scope the exposure. Search the logs for other sessions where the bot returned records that did not belong to the signed-in customer.
  4. Find the root cause. Review how the bot is configured: what it is allowed to look up, and whether user text and system instructions are kept separate.
  5. Escalate. Involve privacy and legal staff, because customer data was exposed. Follow your incident response and notification policy.

Root cause

This is prompt injection leading to data loss and a privacy incident. Two design flaws combined: the bot treated untrusted user text as if it were instructions, and its lookup tool could return any customer’s records, not just the signed-in customer’s.

Controls that limit the damage

  • Least privilege for tools: scope the lookup so it can only return the authenticated customer’s own records, no matter what the model is told.
  • Separate trusted instructions from untrusted content, and treat everything a user or a document supplies as untrusted.
  • Input and output filtering to catch sensitive data before it reaches the screen.
  • Human approval for high-impact actions, and logging and monitoring so abuse is visible quickly.

How the exam may ask about it

Expect a short scenario where an AI tool reveals or does something it should not after crafted input, and you must name the risk (prompt injection) and choose the best control. Note the pattern: the strongest answer limits what the AI can do, because you cannot rely on the model to always resist manipulated text.

Self-check

Question 1. A chatbot that can look up orders reveals another customer’s data after a user message tells it to ignore its rules. Which control would BEST limit the damage if this is attempted again?

A. Retrain the model on more customer conversations
B. Restrict the lookup tool so it can only return the signed-in customer’s own records
C. Ask customers not to type instructions into the chat
D. Increase the chatbot’s response length limit

Show answer and explanation

Correct: B. Least privilege limits what the bot can reach regardless of what text it is given.

Why the others are wrong: A does not fix the architecture and may add more customer data to the risk. C depends on attackers following a request. D has nothing to do with access to records.

Scenario 2: the spam filter that started trusting a campaign

TICKET: SEC-4188 | Priority: Medium | Status: Open
Opened: Friday 14:05 | Reporter: Email Security Analyst
System: AI spam classifier, retrained monthly on user-reported labels
Symptom: After this month’s retrain, messages from one phishing campaign are passing the filter. Detection rate for that campaign fell sharply.

Investigation

  1. Compare model versions. Test last month’s model and this month’s model against the same sample of messages to confirm the change came from the retrain.
  2. Inspect the training data. Look at what changed between datasets, and where the labels came from.
  3. Check label sources. Find a cluster of “not spam” labels submitted by a small number of new accounts for messages from the same campaign.
  4. Roll back. Redeploy last month’s model while the data is cleaned.
  5. Review the change process. Confirm who approved the new training data and what testing happened before release.

Root cause

This is poisoning: the training data was tampered with through an unvetted feedback loop, so the model learned to treat the campaign as legitimate. It is different from evasion, where an attacker crafts a message at detection time to slip past a model that was trained correctly.

Controls that limit the damage

  • Data provenance and validation: know where each label came from and reject or review suspicious clusters.
  • Access control and integrity checks on training data and model files, so changes are authorized and detectable.
  • Versioning and rollback so a bad model can be replaced fast.
  • Change management and testing: a held-out test set and an approval step before any retrained model goes live. This links to the change management objective in Domain 1.

How the exam may ask about it

A question may describe a model whose behavior degrades after a retrain and ask you to identify the risk and the best preventive control. Watch for the clue “training data” or “retrained”: that points to poisoning, while “crafted input at the time of use” points to evasion or prompt injection.

Self-check

Question 2. After retraining on user-reported labels, a spam filter passes one campaign’s messages. Many “not spam” labels came from a few new accounts. Which risk and control fit BEST?

A. Prompt injection; filter user input
B. Poisoning; validate the provenance of training labels and keep model versions for rollback
C. Hallucination; add citations to the model’s output
D. Jailbreaking; tighten the model’s safety prompts

Show answer and explanation

Correct: B. Tampered training labels are poisoning, and provenance checks plus rollback address the root cause.

Why the others are wrong: A and D describe attacks at the time of use, not on training data. C addresses false statements in output, which is not what happened here.

Scenario 3: the urgent call that sounded like the CFO

TICKET: SEC-4203 | Priority: High | Status: Contained
Opened: Monday 16:20 | Reporter: Accounts Payable Supervisor
System: Phone and payments workflow
Symptom: An accounts payable clerk received a voicemail that sounded like the CFO, asking for an urgent wire to a new vendor. A second approver noticed the vendor was not on file and paused the payment.

Investigation

  1. Stop the payment and, if any money moved, contact the bank immediately.
  2. Verify out of band. Call the CFO using a number from the company directory, not the number that left the voicemail.
  3. Preserve evidence: the voicemail file, caller details, emails and the payment request.
  4. Check for other targets. Ask finance and executive-assistant staff whether they received similar messages.
  5. Report and review. Log the incident and review the payment approval process.

Root cause

This is social engineering using a deepfake: synthetic audio imitating a trusted executive to create urgency. The weakness is a process that allowed a payment request to be accepted on the authority of a single voice.

Controls that limit the damage

  • Out-of-band verification through a known, independent channel for any unusual payment or credential request.
  • Dual approval and a strict procedure for new vendors and changed bank details.
  • Awareness training that uses real examples and rewards people for reporting suspicious requests.
  • A no-blame reporting culture, so staff pause and ask instead of rushing.

How the exam may ask about it

Expect an urgent request from a seemingly senior person and a question about the best response or the best preventive control. The answer is almost never “listen more carefully for audio glitches.” It is a process control such as verification through a separate channel.

Self-check

Question 3. An employee receives an urgent voicemail that sounds like the CFO, requesting a wire to a new vendor. Which control would BEST prevent a fraudulent payment?

A. Train staff to detect small audio glitches in voicemails
B. Require verification through a known number and dual approval for new-vendor payments
C. Block all incoming calls from outside the company
D. Replace phone calls with voicemail

Show answer and explanation

Correct: B. Out-of-band verification and dual approval work even if the fake is perfect.

Why the others are wrong: A relies on detection that gets harder as fakes improve. C would disrupt legitimate business without stopping fraud by email or other channels. D does not change who is trusted.

Control mapping: risk, example and control

RiskExampleControl ideas
Prompt injectionChatbot steered by crafted user textLeast-privilege tools, trusted/untrusted separation, filtering, human approval, logging
PoisoningTampered feedback labels used for retrainingData provenance, validation, integrity checks, versioning, rollback, change management
Model manipulationUnauthorized changes to a model or its settingsAccess control, integrity checks such as hashing or signing, monitoring
JailbreakingUser gets a model to ignore safety rulesLayered guardrails, output filtering, monitoring and rate limiting
EvasionInput crafted to slip past a detection modelLayered detection, adversarial testing, not relying on one model
HallucinationsConfident but wrong answers used in a decisionHuman review, verified sources, no unreviewed automation of high-impact decisions
Data loss and privacyStaff paste sensitive data into a public AI toolData classification, DLP-style controls, redaction, policy and training
Session hijacking, code executionAI tool abused to reach sessions or run commandsSecure session handling, MFA, sandboxing, disabling unneeded tools
Bias, explainability, ethicsUnfair or unexplainable automated decisionsGovernance policy, testing, documentation, human oversight, audits
Deepfake fraudSynthetic voice or video imitating an executiveOut-of-band verification, dual approval, awareness training

How to study the AI topics

  1. Make flashcards for the terms, grouped as attacks on the model, attacks through the model, and risks in output and data.
  2. Practice the three-step answer: name the risk, explain the impact, pick the control that limits damage.
  3. Reuse what you already know. Least privilege, change management, hashing and awareness training explain most of the right answers.
  4. Separate similar pairs: poisoning versus evasion, and prompt injection versus jailbreaking.
  5. Check the official objectives for the exact topic list, then test yourself with our Threats and vulnerabilities practice questions.
